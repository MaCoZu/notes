## Accessibility

Things should be designed to be usable, without modification, by as many people as possible.

There are four characteristics of accessible designs:

1. **Perceptibility** — everyone can perceive the design, regardless of sensory abilities.
2. **Operability** — everyone can use the design, regardless of physical abilities.
3. **Simplicity** — everyone can easily learn and understand the design, regardless of experience, literacy, or concentration level.
4. **Forgiveness** — designs minimize the occurrence and consequences of errors.

## Ackoff’s Law

Ackoff’s law states that doing the wrong things, even if perfectly executed, will never be successful. Doing the right things well is more likely to succeed.

> Doing the right things is ‘wisdom’, and doing things right is ‘efficiency’.

## Aesthetic-Usability Effect

Aesthetic things are subjectively perceived as easier to use than ugly ones. Additionally, people are more likely to accept, care for, keep, display, and repeatedly use things that are aesthetic.

## Affordance

Simply put, a thing has good affordance if it does what it looks like.

To be more precise, psychologist James J. Gibson developed the term, describing affordances as the possible ways an animal can interact with its environment, according to its selective and changing needs. For example an infant uses a tooth brush unlike an adult, and by learning the conventional meaning of an object the “child enters into the shared practices of society”.

In his book “The Design of Everyday Things” Donald Norman defines affordances as action possibilities and adds the concept of signifiers to elicit these possibilities.

> “Affordances define what actions are possible. Signifiers specify how people discover those possibilities: signifiers are signs, perceptible signals of what can be done. Signifiers are of far more importance to designers than are affordances.”

## Alignment

The arrangement of elements along a common axis based on their edges, centers, or areas.

Elements are often aligned according to grid systems, while grids don’t have to be rectangular.

Aligned elements appear more stable, cohesive and united, while the users eyes are guided along predictable lines connecting the dots.

While elements are frequently aligned symmetrically by their edges and axes, sometimes it is advised to align by eye, balancing out extending parts or irregular shapes of a design. Like the serif of a capital T. When aligning a T with an M on the left edge, shifting the T slightly to the left, alleviates the empty space between the left edge and the stem of the T.

## Anchoring

The subconscious reaches for reference points (anchors) when making decisions and judgments. Therefore, the order and vicinity of information presented plays a role in many contexts.

For example a car is perceived to be cheaper when placed next to an expensive one (the anchor), even if the two cars are incomparable.

In a range of signals, the first, and last one are regularly taken as the anchors, with the signals in between being ignored.

While most of the research focusses on numbers, the effect applies to all senses. Sounds, arguments, colors all change in light of the reference.

## Box’s Law

Statistician George E. P. Box coined the aphorism “All models are wrong, but some are useful”. Making a case for the utility of statistical models even if they conceal most of the deeper complexities.

## Causal Reductionism

Also called ‘the Fallacy of the Single Cause’.

It is simplification gone wrong. While Box had a point in saying models help us to elicit certain aspects of phenomena, humans have a lazy tendency to smoothen complexities to linear explanations.

Especially when events are characterized by high volatility, ambiguity, or significant negative impact, interconnected, jointly sufficient causes are boiled down to a few convenient, easily identifiable, and often emotionally resonant elements.

## Closure

For visualizations closure means the brain automatically completes recognizable forms when they are incomplete or interrupted.

In psychological terms the need for (cognitive) closure is the desire for a clear, firm answer and aversion to ambiguity.

While the need for closure is positively correlated with stability and clarity it has downsides we touched upon above. Namely, persons with a strong need for closure are more likely to clutch at simple cognitive structures, freeze on early judgments and resist new unsettling information.

Everyone concerned with predictions, from statisticians to political analysts, will look for closure of their models and hypothesis to lend them some clout. This and many other cognitive biases calls our prized rationality into question.

Chances are that you were asked about your need for cognitive closure in some job related context:

> “How do you handle situations where you have incomplete information, and how do you determine when you have 'enough' data to proceed?”
