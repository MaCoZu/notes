People wielding numbers usually have something important to convey, but being numbers persons they do not always package this information palatable for the non-nerd.

Our brains did not evolve to juggle numbers, and certainly not the wildly huge and small figures we have surrounded us with lately. The Nano’s and the Tera’s, who can really comprehend them?

Our short-term memory can hold [7 plus/minus 2](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/gilden/files/2016/04/MagicNumberSeven-Miller1956.pdf) digits, which is about the same, as the number of items we can grasp by just glancing at them. An ability called ‘**subitizing**’.

Confronted with larger numbers, our brains start to save energy. Which corresponds with two types of ‘**numbing**’.

1. **Psychophysical numbing:** We become less sensitive to changes in larger numbers. While the jump from 10 to 20 feels significant, an equal jump from 340 to 350 does not.
2. **Psychic Numbing:** Our empathy diminishes as soon as we consider more than one victim. The fate of one individual feels weighty, but the suffering of thousands blurs into the abstract. (Biased media uses this barbarously.)

Our Stone Age brains favor concrete, whole objects, things we can count with our fingers and sometimes toes, too. In ancient cultures more than 5 equal things just became ‘lots’. And if you wonder why many number systems have the basis of 5 or 10, look no further than your own hands. Everything out of sight or more numerous to subitize belongs to the abstract.

That’s of course unfortunate, in the name of science and rationality we want our peers to understand the numbers and their significance for them. If we happen to have information in numerical form, it falls on us to illustrate the importance of our findings and find way to go around the brain’s inclination to classify numbers into ‘very little’ or ‘a lot’ (who cares?).

Chip Heath and Karla Starr in their book _[Making numbers count: the art and science of communicating numbers](https://www.amazon.com/Making-Numbers-Count-Science-Communicating/dp/1982165448)_, present some fine methods to bring numbers to life.

## Curse of knowledge

Maybe the first thing to remember when presenting statistical or otherwise numerical information, is that your audience is frequently not as versed or occupied with the subject as you are. Forgetting to clarify the context and implications, or dropping scientific terms without further explanation, just to ramble about some naked numbers is a surefire way to lose your audience in a heartbeat.

Experts poring over a problem for month on end, may lose the sense of how little the rest of the world cares about it. Thus, if you don’t want to appear as a mad professor, take a step back and simplify your statements to a point that seems like a caricature. No worries you can add detail after you have initiated your listeners.

## Comparisons

Let’s assume your mother is afraid about dying in a plane crash, and you can not move her to visit you in your new home on the Galápagos Islands. You tell her about the [0.000001% chance](https://flyfright.com/plane-crash-statistics/) of dying in a plane crash to no avail. A chance is chance and our mother is not going to gamble with god. Your mom would have no problem to visit you by car though. Thus, a little comparison comes in handy, you can tell her that driving to the airport terminal is statistically a 1000 more deadly than flying.

Or think about the outrageous costs of military equipment, like the \$143 million for one high-end fighter jet (F-22 Raptor). If you don’t have a good grasp of what 1m of public money can buy, the figure is just smoke and mirrors. The extremely inefficient weapons manufacturers can sell almost anything on behalf of national security, and the Raptor is a [prime example of squander](https://militarymachine.com/f-22-cost).

For the taxpayer concerned with security, it might be of interest that $143 Million can buy 14,300 Feilong-300 D Chinese suicide drones for $10,000 per piece. That is fourteen thousand and three hundredth drones versus one single fighter jet.

For a more peace loving citizen, \$143 Million can be compared with 3 million vaccinated children or 4 apartment complexes, providing homes for 400 families that one could build with this amount of money.
